Bid more. Win more. · Issue 02

Why does every bid feel like starting again?

Your team already knows more than it can find. That is where time, confidence and bidding capacity disappear.

Yorik Tisseau, MBA30 August 2026 · 4 min readFollow on LinkedIn

Every new tender seems to begin with the same expensive ritual: finding work the organisation knows it has done before.

Someone remembers a similar question from two years ago. Someone else is sure there is a strong case study somewhere. The Bid Director knows the team solved this problem on another pursuit, but cannot remember who approved the evidence, whether it is still current or where the final version lives.

Meanwhile, the deadline has already started moving.

It is a horrible feeling. Not because the team lacks experience, but because that experience is always just out of reach when it matters most.

So good people start rebuilding. They search old folders, message former team members and reopen decisions the organisation has already paid to make. Senior attention disappears into reconstructing context before the real questions have even been properly addressed:

Should we bid?

What is our case to win?

What can we actually prove?

By the time the team has assembled enough of the picture, it is already under pressure. The pursuit feels less certain than it should. People are busy, but nobody feels as though the bid has properly started.

The last bid should have made this one easier. Too often, it did not.

That is why capacity is not only a headcount problem. It is also the accumulated cost of asking experienced people to recover knowledge the business already owns.

Individual prompting can help. One curious bid professional may find a document faster, structure a question better or test an idea before taking it to the team. That can be genuinely useful.

But if the result stays in one person's chat history, the organisation has not learned anything. The next person still starts again. The next tender still depends on who remembers what, who happens to be available and how quickly they can find it.

The real shift is from helping one person in the moment to giving the whole team a stronger place to begin.

Imagine opening a new tender and, before the first strategy meeting, seeing the relevant requirements alongside the evidence used previously, the decisions made, the lessons learned and the gaps that still need an honest answer.

Not a finished response. Not an invented win theme. A prepared starting point the team can question.

That distinction matters. Bid leaders do not need a system to make the judgement for them. They need enough of the right information in the room, early enough, to make that judgement well.

When that happens, the mood of the pursuit changes. The first meeting is not a scavenger hunt. Senior people can spend their time deciding whether to pursue, where the team can genuinely differentiate, which evidence will survive scrutiny and what needs to be fixed before it becomes a late-bid crisis.

That creates room to pursue more opportunities without simply asking everyone to work harder. It also improves the quality of the opportunities already being pursued, because the team has more time to shape, challenge and assure the case to win.

This is what usable institutional memory feels like: less panic, fewer repeated conversations and more confidence that the team is building on what came before.

It needs governance. Someone must decide what information is approved, what can be reused, what an automated step may retrieve or flag, and where a person must review and approve the result. The team remains accountable for the decision to bid, the strategy to win and every claim submitted.

The organisations that make progress will not necessarily be the ones with the most AI. They will be the ones that stop allowing valuable bid knowledge to disappear at the end of every pursuit.

Because the question is not whether your team knows enough.

It is whether they can use what they know before the deadline takes over.
