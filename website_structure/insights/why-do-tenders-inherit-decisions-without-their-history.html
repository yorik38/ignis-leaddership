Why do tenders inherit decisions without their history?

When commercial memory breaks, capable people spend the deadline reconstructing the past instead of shaping what comes next.

Yorik Tisseau, MBA21 September 2026 · 5 min readFollow on LinkedIn

Know someone who would value this? Share it with your network.

I have sat in enough tender kick-offs to recognise the moment.

Everyone arrives ready to move. The opportunity is exciting, the deadline is real and capable people are already thinking about the work ahead.

Then someone asks a simple question.

Why did we choose this route?

The room hesitates, not because nobody knows, but because the answer is scattered. Part of it sits in an early model, part came from a conversation months ago, and the person who understood the concern best is not in the room.

Of course the team will find the answer, but by the time it does, finding it has already become part of the tender.

Months later, the same unease returns when a commercial assumption is sitting inside a price and finance, legal or delivery wants to know how the business arrived there.

And there is a cost to it. Good people feel exposed, senior leaders lose confidence in what is in front of them, and the team becomes defensive just when it most needs to think clearly.

Nobody has failed, but the business has lost part of its commercial memory.

Why is the tender only the middle?

The tender is only the middle because it inherits decisions made before it and turns them into commitments people will live with afterwards. By the time the tender starts, routes, dates and risks have already been discussed; by the time it ends, those choices have become scope, price and promises that must survive negotiation and close.

A tender is not an isolated island; it is the bridge between the opportunity people imagined and the deal the organisation may eventually sign.

That bridge needs more than documents; it needs commercial memory, a usable record of what the organisation believed, why it believed it, what changed and who has the authority to make the next commitment.

Without that memory, a provisional route can be repeated until it sounds settled, an optimistic date can slip into the plan, and a concern meant to be revisited can survive into the offer because everybody assumes somebody else resolved it.

What does weak commercial memory cost?

Weak commercial memory costs more than the time spent searching for the latest file. It forces capable people to reopen settled questions, delays decisions while they reconcile different versions and allows assumptions to travel further than the evidence behind them. The visible cost is repeated work; the deeper cost is weaker confidence when the organisation needs to commit.

You can feel that cost in the room as a confident discussion becomes cautious and people start checking old messages. Somebody says, “I think that was agreed,” only for somebody else to ask, “Agreed by whom?”

The pressure lands hardest on the people who can remember. They become the unofficial thread running through the opportunity, which works until they are unavailable, stretched across several priorities or asked to recall a decision made months earlier under completely different conditions.

Strong commercial memory changes what happens next because the team spends less time searching and reconciling, while leaders can see the evidence, uncertainty and ownership around a decision before they are asked to make it.

That is what commercial excellence should feel like: not a perfect process map, but informed decisions made with enough of the history in view. People can challenge a position without first reconstructing it, and uncertainty becomes visible early enough for someone to accept it, change it or stop the work before it hardens into a commitment.

Why does agentic AI make this practical now?

This is where I am genuinely excited by agentic AI because, in 2026, many of the pieces needed to build commercial memory are available. Agents can retrieve from enterprise sources, retain working context, use tools and expose their activity for review, creating an opportunity to connect those capabilities around a commercial decision while keeping authority with people.

Imagine a commercial review that does not begin with ten minutes of asking which file is current.

The original assumption, the evidence behind it and the outstanding decision are already visible, leaving the people in the room to concentrate on the part that requires experience and courage.

Or imagine a clarification changing an assumption agreed months earlier. Instead of relying on the one person who remembers, the team can see where the assumption entered the solution, what else it touched and who now needs to decide.

By the final approval meeting, the numbers may look settled even though the evidence beneath them has moved. An agent can bring that history and the unresolved conditions into the room before a leader is asked to commit.

And this only earns trust if people can see where an answer came from, where uncertainty remains and who is expected to make the call. Otherwise, the agent does not strengthen commercial memory; it simply hides the gap behind a more confident answer.

Used well, this changes the feeling in the room because people are no longer forced to defend fragments they cannot fully trace. They can look at the same history, challenge it together and decide what should happen next.

Where should a commercial leader start?

Start where commercial memory is already failing. Most leaders know the place: the review that always overruns, the assumption everyone handles carefully or the handover followed by private messages. Choose one recent opportunity and ask what the next team had to reconstruct before it could make a sound decision.

Three questions are enough:

  1. What did the next team have to rediscover?
  2. Which assumption changed without a clear reason or owner?
  3. What could a system prepare while a named person still makes the decision?

The answer may be a better decision record, a clearer ownership rule or one bounded human-agent workflow. What matters is that the organisation stops asking its best people to carry commercial memory in their heads and rebuild it whenever the work changes hands.

The tender is only the middle, but it should not be the moment when the organisation starts remembering how the deal began, because every hour spent looking backwards is an hour the team cannot spend shaping what comes next.
