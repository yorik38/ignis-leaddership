A pause, not a stop: what has to change before AR9

What sits behind the pause, and what a redesigned round would ask of energy developers.

Yorik TisseauFounder, Ignis LeadershipMonday 9 November 2026

Know someone who would value this? Share it with your network.

The government has not halted renewable subsidies. AR8 is still running. What it has paused is the design of the next round, and the reasons tell energy developers what AR9 is likely to ask of them.

The short version: On 3 November the energy minister, Michael Shanks, told peers the government wants to "pause before AR9" and look at whether the Contracts for Difference scheme needs "more significant changes" to work for consumers. AR8 carries on, with sealed bids expected in December and results expected in February 2027. Nothing about AR9's design, timing or budget has been published. But the problem it is being redesigned around is on the record: a grid that cannot carry power from where it is built, constraint costs that the National Audit Office puts at £1.9 billion in 2025-26 and up to £7.8 billion by 2030, and a national plan for where generation should go that will not be final until the end of 2027.

What was actually said

The line came late in a Lords session on low-wind, low-sun weather. The minister first defended the scheme: "The CfD process has been incredibly useful in giving investor certainty to build new projects," and "We think it is the useful model at the moment." Then: "AR7 and AR8 ran on broadly the same terms as previous auction rounds. We now want to pause before AR9 and look at whether we need some more significant changes to the contracts for difference scheme to make sure that they work for the consumers of the country."1

That is the whole statement. The transcript is uncorrected, and witnesses can still send minor corrections.

The Telegraph read it as a halt to new wind and solar subsidies, though its own report says AR8 "is expected to continue as normal."2 Energy Voice and Utility Week reported a pause to look at reform.34 Ørsted's chief executive, Rasmus Errboe, said he did not see it as a pause.5 UKSIF's James Alexander said the comments would "deeply concern" investors and called for "urgent clarity."3

One thing nobody official has said: what "pause" means for the calendar. The minister gave no date for AR9 and did not say it would open later than planned. Some trade titles call it a delay. Until DESNZ publishes something, that is a reading, not a fact.

How we got here

The CfD is the government's main support mechanism for new low carbon generation in Great Britain.18 AR5 secured no offshore wind. AR7, announced on 14 January 2026, secured a record 8.4 gigawatts, with fixed-bottom offshore wind clearing at £91.20 per megawatt hour in England and Wales and £89.49 in Scotland, in 2024 prices.67

Then the test changed. The Budget on 28 October said the government will procure new generation "only where it lowers bills, benefits the system, and can be accommodated by the grid." The same document said: "Further reforms to the Contracts for Difference scheme will be delivered before the opening of Allocation Round 9."8 So reform before AR9 was already policy. Shanks's remark put a pause around it.

The chancellor, John Healey, made the logic plain in The Times: "There's no sense building power that can't connect and then paying to switch it off."9

The bill behind that sentence is real. The National Audit Office reported in September that constraint costs, the payments to turn generators down or up when the grid cannot carry the power, totalled £1.9 billion in 2025-26 and "could increase up to £7.8 billion by 2030 without concerted action." Of the 80 grid projects NESO said were needed for clean power, 64 are under way and most are at an early stage.10

The minister was candid about where the fault lies. "Far be it for me to defend the developers, but it is not their fault that the generation is not getting to where it needs to get to. That is the fault of the grid." And, speaking as a Scottish MP who lives next to two of Europe's biggest onshore wind farms: "if we were logically designing a system, we may not have placed quite so many wind farms quite so far away from centres of demand."1

What is going on with AR8

AR8 is live, and it is the last round the minister described as running on familiar terms. Applications closed in August and stay confidential, so we do not yet know who is in.

The shape of the offer is public. There are five pots: solar; onshore and remote-island wind; fixed-bottom offshore wind; floating and other deepwater offshore wind; and a fifth pot for emerging technologies such as tidal and wave. Ceiling prices, the administrative strike prices, are unchanged from AR7 in 2024 terms: £75 per megawatt hour for solar, £92 for onshore and remote-island wind, £113 for offshore wind, and £271 for floating and other deepwater offshore wind.22 Those are ceilings, not clearing prices.

The pound budget for each pot is still unpublished. That is by design. DESNZ said it would set the figures after applications were assessed, with value for money as a key test, and that the large offshore wind budget increase in AR7 should not be taken as a guide this time. An increase in one of the main pots may reduce the chance of more support in another.22 The initial budgets land on 19 November, in the Contract Budget Notice, alongside NESO's Notice of Auction. Indicative dates after that are sealed bids from 3 to 9 December and results from 15 to 17 February 2027, until that notice confirms them.1213

AR8 is already stricter on grid readiness than past rounds: applicants generally need a Gate 2 connection agreement, with only certain Gate 1 reservation projects allowed.14 In the fixed-bottom offshore pot, projects already clear under two prices, split by where they connect.22 The pause changes how the round feels, not its rules. That is a reason to stay clear-eyed, not a reason to bid below a financeable level.15

The real challenge: a contract that does not know where it is

Today's CfD pays on actual metered output, whenever the reference price is zero or above. Contracts from AR4 onwards get no difference payment in hours when the day-ahead price is negative.17 In 2025 there were 168 negative price hours, 1.9% of the year.16 What the contract does not do, on its own, is ask whether the power can get anywhere. The location signal comes from connections and network charges.

The government has been building a new frame around it, in pieces:

  • The Strategic Spatial Energy Plan. NESO has given DESNZ six pathways. The minister told peers the chosen pathway should be public in the first quarter of 2027, consulted on, with a final decision at the end of 2027.1
  • Reformed national pricing. The April 2026 delivery plan lists the CfD as one of the "siting and investment levers." Government will "consider additional locational elements" in support schemes, has ruled out a deemed CfD, is still looking at a capacity-based CfD, and says it does not intend to change CfDs already issued or existing connection offers.17
  • The Cost Control Framework. Due at the 2027 spending review. Until it is in place, "no further policy costs will be placed on bills unless they bring bills down or are essential for security of supply."8

Put those together and the reason for a pause is easier to see. Our reading, not a government statement: AR9 may be the first round asked to carry a location and system-value signal, while the map it should follow and the cost envelope it must fit are both still being drawn.

What a redesigned AR9 would have to weigh

These are not predictions. They are the options and tensions already on the public record.

  1. Where. A locational element in the auction, aligned to plan zones and to the grid capacity available in each. That rewards projects in the right places. It also strands development spend on sites chosen under the old signals, and the "no retroactive change" promise covers issued contracts, not projects still in development.
  2. What it pays for. Output-based payment is described as "robust." A capacity-based CfD is still under consideration.17 A change of payment basis moves risk between generator and consumer, and lenders will price it.
  3. Shape and storage. The committee chair raised "shaping requirements" that would push developers towards storage, quoting Andy McNeill of the Energy Storage Association. The minister: "It sounds like a sensible suggestion," while noting storage "does not have to be directly linked" to generation.1
  4. The bill test. "Lowers bills" is the stated criterion. How it will be scored for an individual bid has not been published.
  5. Investability. The CfD's value is revenue certainty. A long or undated pause is a risk premium on every pipeline that was planning for AR9. A short, dated one is a design window.
  6. The rest of the cost stack. The Energy Independence Bill would add mandatory community funds and a larger community investment offer for some projects (see the flags below). AR9 bids would price those in.

How it moves forward

Today there is no AR9 consultation and no AR9 timetable. The Budget commits only that reforms come before AR9 opens.

The last two rounds took roughly five to seven months from consultation to application window, for changes the government itself called refinements.1819 "More significant changes" would very likely take longer. That is our inference from the precedent, not a published plan.

The places energy developers will get a say are already visible: the AR9 consultation when it lands, the SSEP pathway consultation in early 2027, the reformed national pricing decisions, the 10 Year Plan, and the 2027 spending review. Four things would turn a pause into a plan: a dated AR9 consultation, a clear list of what is in scope, an indicative AR9 window, and early sight of how plan zones would apply to auction eligibility.

What it means for energy developers

  1. In AR8: bid on the terms you know. A redesigned AR9 is a reason to be clear-eyed, not a reason to bid below a financeable level.
  2. Targeting AR9: map each project against connection evidence, constraint exposure and the likely SSEP zones. Keep a credible PPA or merchant fallback.
  3. Boards and investors: run a case where AR9 opens later than planned and on different terms. Know which projects survive it.
  4. When the consultation lands: respond with evidence. Location, shape and cost data will move the design more than adjectives.

Also this week

Ofgem wants energy developers to build more of their own transmission connection. A consultation open until 13 November would let generation, storage and demand customers self-build the sole-use assets from their site to the existing transmission system, then transfer them to the Transmission Owner.20 That is not the same as the offshore OFTO model: the asset ends up with the onshore network owner. Wider and enabling works stay out of scope, and connection dates are often driven by those works. A decision is expected in the first quarter of 2027.

Community funds move from voluntary towards mandatory. The Energy Independence Bill, introduced on 4 November, has two separate measures.21 An unused 2015 power could require an investment offer worth up to the equivalent of 20% of development capital costs, up from 5%. It is an offer to invest, not free equity. Separately, new onshore wind and ground-mounted solar over 5 MW would have to sign a legally binding community benefit agreement, with minded-to minimums of £5,000 per MW a year for wind and £700 for solar. Both still need secondary legislation. For host communities this means more predictable funding and a chance to invest. For energy developers the extra cost and process are real.

If AR9 had to tell you where to build, would your pipeline survive the map?
