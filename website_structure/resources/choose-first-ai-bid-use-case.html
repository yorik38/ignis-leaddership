Where should bid teams start with AI agents?

Start with valuable work, then test the smallest useful intervention.

Yorik Tisseau, MBA10 September 2026 · 11 min read

Share this practical guide.

Every polished demonstration works. The tender parses cleanly, the evidence library was curated for the session and no live deadline depends on the output.

A demonstration proves that a capability exists. It does not prove that the capability can survive your real workflow: the scanned addendum, conflicting evidence, absent subject matter expert, unclear owner and fixed submission deadline.

The first use case should therefore be chosen by scoring the work, not watching the software. The aim is to find one bounded problem that matters, can be evaluated and leaves a named person in control. If the conditions are not ready, the right first investment may be process or knowledge work rather than an AI pilot.

Key takeaways

  1. Choose a business problem, not an AI feature. Name who experiences it, when it occurs and which consequential decision it weakens.
  2. Reject what cannot be evaluated or controlled. A high-value use case with no reliable check or recovery owner is not ready, whatever its headline score.
  3. Build evidence before scale. Establish the baseline, run in shadow mode, measure review burden and connect the output to a downstream decision.

Step 1. Write the problem without naming the solution

A useful problem statement describes what happens, to whom, when and which later decision suffers. It contains no technology.

Weak: We should use AI to speed up proposal writing.

That is a solution wearing a problem’s clothing. It cannot be scored because it names no consequence.

Stronger: Bid reviewers spend the first day of review checking whether responses address every tender requirement and whether important claims are supported by approved evidence. That crowds out review of argument quality and commercial risk at the point in the schedule where there is least time to fix anything.

The stronger version identifies the user, trigger, recurring friction and decision that suffers. It also keeps the intervention open. The problem might need a process change, a deterministic rule, an automated handover, bounded AI assistance or a controlled multi-step workflow.

Reflection Who experiences this problem, when does it occur, and which later decision is weakened by it?

Step 2. Score five conditions out of ten

Score each condition from 0 to 2. Use evidence from recent pursuits rather than optimism about the proposed solution.

8 to 10: a strong candidate for the smallest credible test. 5 to 7: conditional, so identify the weak condition and fix it first. Below 5: not ready. The honest output is a foundations plan, not a pilot.

Two stop rules override the total. Reject a use case that scores 0 on evaluability or 0 on human control and recovery. If nobody can reliably check the output or own the failure, a persuasive demonstration only increases the risk that weak work travels further before it is challenged.

Context readiness is the condition teams are most tempted to round up. Test it concretely. Pick one important claim and ask a knowledgeable person to locate the approved source. If the answer depends on who happens to be available, the context is not ready.

Reflection Which weak condition could make a convincing demonstration unsafe or irrelevant in real work?

Step 3. Check the simpler alternative

Before approving a pilot, walk down the intervention ladder. Could a clearer owner, earlier meeting or removed handover solve it? Could an exact rule handle it? Could a conventional integration move the information reliably?

Use AI where language, ambiguity, variable structure or synthesis across sources makes it useful. Use rules where the result must be exact and identical every time. Page limits, mandatory annexes, format validation and certificate dates are deterministic jobs.

Record why the simpler option was rejected. If that reasoning cannot be written down, the organisation has selected technology rather than made an investment decision.

Reflection What is the lowest-cost intervention that can solve the problem dependably?

Compare candidates, not demonstrations

The scores below are illustrative. Your evidence may produce a different answer.

Evidence retrieval for response planning8Test narrowlyThe workflow depends on evidence ownership and provenance, not retrieval alone
Capture pack assembly7Fix foundations firstCommercial value may be high, but fragmented and permission-sensitive context weakens reliability
Autonomous Go or No-Go5, rejectedRedesignEvaluability and human control score zero when the system replaces the accountable decision

The comparison exposes an important distinction: the most valuable use case is not always the right first use case.

Capture intelligence and qualification can materially affect where the business spends its scarce pursuit capacity. But the relevant information often lives across CRM notes, account history, call records and human judgement. Requirements extraction may have a smaller strategic headline, yet it begins with a complete tender pack, has a known reviewer and can be compared against a human baseline.

The rejected Go or No-Go example should not be abandoned. Redesign it. Let the workflow assemble requirements, capability evidence, capacity conflicts, risks and comparable outcomes into a decision pack delivered before the qualification meeting. The AI improves the decision input and timing. The Sponsor retains the decision.

Reflection Are we selecting the use case with the greatest theoretical value, or the one that can produce the strongest evidence for the next investment?

Establish the baseline before the pilot

Without a baseline, the pilot can only produce impressions.

Take several recent pursuits and reconstruct the current task. Record who performed it, elapsed and active effort, corrections, exceptions, reviewer involvement and what escaped into later work. The baseline will not be perfect. Approximate and documented is more useful than precise and imagined.

Measure the whole workflow. A system that saves an author four hours and adds three hours of senior review has not saved four hours. It has moved cost to the more constrained person. Review burden, context preparation and downstream rework belong in the baseline and the result.

This is the thought-leadership test for any productivity claim: where did the work go? If the answer is “into review”, the workflow has not created the capacity the Sponsor thinks they bought.

Reflection What do we know about current performance, and what are we only assuming?

Write the pilot in one sentence

If the pilot cannot be written in one sentence, its boundary is not agreed.

When [named user] reaches [workflow trigger], the workflow will [bounded contribution] using [authorised context] to improve [observable result]. It will stop when [failure condition], and [named person] will decide [human gate].

For requirements extraction:

When the bid manager receives the complete tender pack, the workflow will extract stated requirements into a structured baseline with a source reference against each line, using only the tender documents and addenda, to reduce the review effort spent checking coverage. It will stop when source quality prevents reliable extraction, and the bid manager will approve the baseline before work packages are issued.

The sentence forces agreement on the trigger, contribution, context boundary, observable result, stop condition and accountable handback. A disagreement about the sentence is useful. It reveals a governance gap before it becomes a technical defect.

Run the smallest credible test

A practical 30, 60, 90 structure keeps the test bounded without rushing the evidence. It is a planning frame, not a universal timetable.

  1. Days 1 to 30: baseline and boundaries. Confirm the current measure, authorised sources, test set, edge cases, stop conditions and scale criteria.
  2. Days 31 to 60: shadow mode. Run the workflow beside the existing process on real work. Let it produce output without changing the live process. Classify each correction so you learn whether the weakness sits in the source, rule, prompt or workflow.
  3. Days 61 to 90: decide. Compare the results with the baseline, including review burden and downstream rework. Make an explicit decision to scale, revise or stop against criteria written before the result was known.

Measure completeness, accuracy, unsupported claims, correction types, exceptions, elapsed effort, reviewer effort and downstream rework. Do not replace them with a satisfaction score.

Reflection What result would justify the next test, and what finding would make us stop?

Five ways first pilots fail

The unscored favourite. A senior colleague sees a demonstration and the scorecard is completed afterwards to justify the choice.

The missing baseline. Results are reported as impressions, so nobody can say what changed or defend the next investment.

The context mirage. A curated sample works, live sources do not, and a knowledge ownership problem is misdiagnosed as a model problem.

The hidden burden. Production time falls while review and exception handling rise. The people reporting the saving do not absorb the added work.

The orphan workflow. The output is manually copied into the process it was meant to improve. The capability works but the handover does not.

Four of these are measurement, context or governance failures. That is the pattern worth recognising before another tool is blamed.

Connect the pilot to the practice

A pilot that improves one task and changes nothing downstream has proved a capability, not created an operating improvement.

Before the test ends, decide which downstream step consumes the approved output, who owns that handover and what happens to corrections. Does a correction update a source, a rule, a prompt or the process? Who is authorised to make that change?

For the requirements example, the approved baseline should be reusable by the compliance view, SME work packages and final completeness check. If each still runs from a separately maintained spreadsheet, the pilot has added another step.

The first use case is an investment decision

The Sponsor needs a defensible case for the next increment. The Champion needs the work to become genuinely easier without hidden effort landing on the team. Choose a first use case that lets you answer both.

That is why selection happens after the process map and lifecycle portfolio. The first use case should show that one governed workflow can improve and reveal what must change before the next connection is earned.
