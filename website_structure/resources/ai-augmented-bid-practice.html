How to build an AI-augmented bid practice

Redesign the business-to-bid workflow before deciding where AI belongs.

Yorik Tisseau, MBA10 September 2026 · 10 min read

Share this practical guide.

The first question is often, “Which AI tool should we use?” The better question is: “Which bid decision or handover needs to improve?”

If qualification is late, solution decisions are unclear, evidence is difficult to find and reviewers receive work without the context they need, adding AI to proposal production will not repair the practice. It may help more work reach the same bottlenecks faster.

An AI-augmented bid practice is not your current process with an agent plugged into it. It is a redesigned business-to-bid workflow in which people, rules, automation and AI each have a deliberate role.

The unit of change is therefore the workflow, not the tool. The six moves below take one real pursuit from business outcome through capture, qualification, solution, proposal, handover and learning. Each produces an artefact that business, bid and technical leaders can inspect together.

Key takeaways

  1. Redesign the decision chain before automating a task. The value may sit before or after proposal production, in qualification, context, approval or handover.
  2. Give people, rules and AI different jobs. AI prepares and challenges, rules enforce what must be exact, and named people retain consequential judgement.
  3. Make every workflow earn its next connection. Test against a baseline, include the review burden and scale only when downstream work improves.

1. Define the business outcome

Start above the bid process.

What must the organisation do better? Qualify earlier? Protect scarce bid capacity? Make solution and commercial risk visible sooner? Reuse stronger evidence? Transfer commitments into delivery without reconstructing the bid?

“Use AI in bids” is not an outcome. It cannot be evaluated, funded or stopped. Turn the ambition into one observable workflow problem. “Give the qualification meeting a complete evidence pack before capacity is committed” is a useful problem. “Reduce the time reviewers spend finding unsupported claims” is another.

Name two roles. The Sponsor owns the business consequence, budget and stop decision. The Champion owns or understands the work and can distinguish a useful workflow from a convincing demonstration. A Sponsor without a Champion can fund something the team will not use. A Champion without a Sponsor can create something useful that never becomes an organisational capability.

Artefact: a one-page outcome statement naming the decision to improve, its owner, the current evidence and the intended measure.

Reflection Which business decision should improve, who owns it, and what would show that it has improved?

2. Map the real business-to-bid flow

Do not begin with the procedure manual. Follow three or four recent pursuits, including one that was lost and one the organisation should perhaps not have bid.

Map the actual work from market signal and capture through qualification, strategy, solution, commercial development, proposal production, assurance, submission, delivery handover and learning. Look for:

  • wait points where work is held for information or approval;
  • repeated work and content recreated because the trusted version cannot be found;
  • context lost between business development, bid, solution and delivery teams;
  • decisions made after downstream work has already started;
  • informal workarounds that keep the pursuit moving; and
  • variations that reveal where the standard is unclear.

The map changes the conversation. Instead of asking where an agent might fit, you can ask which delay, handover or decision creates a business consequence. Some of the best opportunities will require no AI at all. That is a useful finding, not a failed AI workshop.

Artefact: one real pursuit mapped as swimlanes, annotated with waits, rework, context loss, decision points and rough effort.

Reflection Where does work wait, repeat, lose context or advance before a decision is ready?

3. Make the decision architecture visible

A bid is not a document production line. It is a chain of consequential decisions: pursue or decline, agree the strategy, choose solution and price trade-offs, accept risk, approve readiness, authorise submission and transfer commitments to delivery.

For each gate, write down four things:

  1. who may say yes, no or not yet;
  2. what must be visible before they decide;
  3. what quality or evidence threshold applies; and
  4. what happens when the threshold is not met.

Then separate three contributions. AI can prepare by extracting, assembling, retrieving, comparing or challenging. Rules can decide what must be exact, such as page limits, mandatory fields, certificate dates and submission formats. People must judge commercial risk, relationship strength, strategy, price, delivery credibility and final commitment.

This avoids two opposite errors. The first is treating an assembled output as a decision because it looks complete. The second is inserting approval at every micro-step, which recreates the bottleneck and leaves the reviewer with no defined question to answer.

Artefact: a decision register with the owner, evidence requirement, threshold, timing and escalation route for every consequential gate.

Reflection Who can say no, and what would have to be in front of them for that no to stick?

4. Design the context before the agent

Most weak AI output is diagnosed as a model or prompting problem when the real issue is context. The system read an outdated case study, an unapproved response, the wrong tender version or a source that nobody owned.

For the chosen workflow, identify the minimum context required: tender documents, customer and opportunity information, approved evidence, solution assumptions, risk and commitment registers, commercial policy, delivery performance and relevant lessons.

For each source, settle access, ownership, currency, approved status and precedence when two sources disagree. Then make traceability a design requirement. A reviewer should be able to move from an output to the source that supports it without beginning a separate investigation.

Keep the boundary tight. A first workflow rarely needs every bid, customer and system. It needs the smallest authorised context that supports one useful job. This is where bid-domain, technical, security and information owners need to work together. Domain leaders define credible bid work. Technical specialists make access and controls reliable. Neither can design the complete capability alone.

Artefact: a context register listing each source, owner, refresh rule, access permission, approved status and traceability method.

Reflection What may this workflow read, who authorises that access, and how will a reviewer check an answer against the original?

5. Choose the simplest intervention that works

There is an intervention ladder, and AI is not the first rung.

  1. Repair the process. Move a late meeting, clarify ownership or remove a duplicate handover.
  2. Write a rule. Use deterministic logic where the result must be exact and repeatable.
  3. Automate the handover. Move structured information reliably between people or systems.
  4. Add bounded AI assistance. Let a person trigger extraction, retrieval, comparison or challenge against approved inputs.
  5. Build a bounded workflow. Coordinate several steps with explicit state, stop conditions and a named human gate.

Choose the lowest rung that addresses the problem dependably. Using a probabilistic system to check a file name, page limit or certificate date adds uncertainty to a job that should be exact.

Requirements extraction shows how the rungs can work together. AI can interpret prose in a tender pack and prepare a source-referenced requirements baseline. A bid manager reviews and approves it once. Deterministic steps can then populate the compliance view, allocate work packages and flag unanswered requirements. The value is not the extraction alone. It is the trusted handover that makes later steps simpler and more consistent.

Artefact: an intervention decision that records the chosen rung and why the simpler option was insufficient.

Reflection Can we defend rejecting the rung below the solution we are proposing?

6. Test the workflow and earn the next connection

Run the workflow alongside the current process before relying on it. Shadow mode gives you a comparison without allowing an unproven output to change live work.

Test representative cases, awkward edge cases, conflicting evidence and poor-quality inputs. Record completeness, accuracy, unsupported claims, corrections, exception handling, elapsed time and downstream rework. Include the effort required to prepare context and review the output.

That last measure matters. A workflow that saves four hours in production and adds three hours of senior review has not saved four hours. It has moved the burden to the more constrained person. The correct unit of value is the workflow outcome, not the speed of the AI step.

Agree stop and scale conditions before seeing the result. Decide who investigates a failure, how the team recovers and whether a correction changes a source, a rule, a prompt or the process. At the end, choose deliberately: stop, repair, refine, repeat or connect the next workflow.

Artefact: an evaluation log with the baseline, test set, corrections, exceptions, review burden and recorded scale or stop decision.

Reflection What improved, what new burden appeared, and has this workflow earned more scope?

The output is a blueprint, not an agent list

Six moves produce six useful artefacts: the outcome statement, real process map, decision register, context register, intervention decision and evaluation plan. Together they form the blueprint for an AI-augmented bid practice.

That blueprint gives technical specialists something implementable and gives business and bid leaders something they can challenge. It also protects the organisation from two expensive errors: buying a tool before defining the work, and building a good pilot that remains disconnected from the decisions it was meant to improve.

Good progress after a year is not “more autonomy”. It is earlier, better-evidenced qualification; one trusted requirements baseline reused through the pursuit; reviewers spending more time on argument and risk than on reconstruction; commitments arriving in delivery with owners and sources; and leaders able to explain what changed against a baseline.

The durable asset is not the interface. It is the operating model, governed context and evidence standard that let the organisation change tools without relearning how the work should happen.
