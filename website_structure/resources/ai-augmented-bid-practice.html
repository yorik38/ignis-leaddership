How to build an AI-augmented bid practice

Redesign the business-to-bid workflow before deciding where AI belongs.

Yorik Tisseau, MBA10 September 2026 · 11 min read

Share this practical guide.

The first question is often, “Which AI tool should we use?” That question comes too early.

If qualification is late, solution decisions are unclear, evidence is difficult to find and reviewers receive work without the context they need, adding AI to proposal production will not repair the bid practice. It may simply help more work reach the same bottlenecks faster.

An AI-augmented bid practice is not your current process with an agent plugged into it. It is a redesigned business-to-bid workflow in which people, rules, automation and AI each have a deliberate role.

The six moves below are not six AI features. They are a practical deployment method for business development and bid management. Apply them across one real pursuit, from the commercial outcome and opportunity decision through solution development, proposal delivery, handover and learning.

1. Define the business outcome

Start above the bid process. What must the organisation do better? Pursue fewer poor-fit opportunities? Protect scarce bid capacity? Bring risk and solution trade-offs into view earlier? Reuse credible evidence? Transfer commitments into delivery without reconstructing the bid?

Turn that ambition into one observable workflow problem. “Use AI in bids” is not an outcome. “Reduce the time reviewers spend finding unsupported claims” is closer. So is “give the qualification meeting a complete evidence pack before capacity is committed”.

Name the Sponsor accountable for the consequence and the Champion who knows how the work really happens. The Sponsor will care about capacity, risk and investment. The Champion will see chasing, workarounds, weak handoffs and repeated decisions. You need both views.

Ask Which business decision should improve, who owns it, and what evidence would show that the workflow is better?

2. Map the real business-to-bid flow

Do not begin with the procedure manual. Follow one live or recently completed pursuit.

Map the work from market signal and capture through qualification, strategy, solution, commercial development, proposal production, assurance, submission, delivery handover and lessons. Record what actually happened:

  • where work waited for information or approval;
  • where people recreated material that already existed;
  • where context was copied between systems or lost in a handover;
  • where a decision arrived after downstream work had started;
  • where an informal workaround kept the pursuit moving; and
  • where the formal process and the working process diverged.

This is where useful AI opportunities appear. Not as a list of generic agents, but as specific points where a better flow could improve a decision, remove avoidable coordination or preserve context.

Ask Where does work wait, repeat, lose context or move forward before a decision is ready?

3. Make the decision architecture visible

A bid workflow is a chain of decisions, not a chain of documents.

For each consequential decision, record the owner, required evidence, quality threshold and response when evidence is incomplete. Typical gates include qualification, win strategy, solution approval, commercial and contractual risk, proposal readiness, final submission and delivery acceptance.

Then separate three contributions:

  1. AI can assemble, compare, retrieve, classify, challenge or prepare.
  2. Deterministic rules can route, validate, calculate or enforce a known condition.
  3. A named person remains accountable for judgement, exceptions and commitments.

This prevents two common design errors: giving AI authority because it can produce a convincing output, and inserting human approval everywhere without deciding what the reviewer must actually judge.

Ask Who can say yes, no or not yet, and what must be visible before they decide?

4. Design the context before the agent

An agent cannot compensate for unclear evidence ownership.

For the chosen workflow, identify the minimum context needed to do dependable work: tender documents, customer and opportunity information, approved case studies, solution assumptions, risk registers, commercial rules, past performance and relevant lessons. For each source, define access, currency, ownership and precedence when two sources conflict.

Keep the boundary tight. The first workflow does not need access to every bid and every system. It needs the smallest authorised context that supports the job, plus a trace showing what evidence informed the output.

This is also the point to involve technology, security and information owners. Domain leaders define what credible bid work looks like. Technical specialists make the connections, permissions and controls reliable. Neither can design the complete system alone.

Ask What may the workflow access, trust and reuse, and how will a reviewer trace the answer to its source?

5. Choose the simplest intervention that works

Some problems need a clearer meeting, owner or rule. Some need deterministic automation. Some suit an AI assistant that retrieves or compares information. Others justify a bounded agent that can use approved tools, maintain state and prepare a handover.

Use AI where language, ambiguity or variable context makes it useful. Give each agent one bounded contribution, explicit inputs and outputs, a stopping condition and a named human handback. If several specialist roles need shared context or sequencing, design the orchestration around the target workflow. Do not make orchestration the starting objective.

A practical first design might connect requirements extraction to a reviewed compliance baseline, then use the approved requirements to prepare SME work packages. The value sits in the handover and preserved context, not in the number of agents.

Ask Is this best solved by process repair, a rule, conventional automation, AI assistance or a bounded agent, and why?

6. Test the workflow and earn the next connection

Run the new workflow beside the current one before relying on it.

Use representative cases, difficult edge cases, conflicting evidence and deliberately poor inputs. Record completeness, accuracy, unsupported claims, reviewer corrections, exception handling, elapsed time and rework. Include the time people spend preparing context and reviewing outputs. A faster draft is not useful if review effort or risk rises elsewhere.

Agree the failure response before the test begins. When should the workflow stop? Who investigates? Can the team recover without losing the original evidence or decision record?

At the end, choose deliberately: stop, repair the process, improve the context, refine the agent, repeat the test or connect the next workflow. Scale is a decision earned by evidence, not the default end of a pilot.

Ask What did the test improve, what new burden did it create, and has it earned more scope?

The output is a blueprint, not an agent list

After these six moves, you should have one practical blueprint containing the business outcome, workflow problem, current flow, decision rights, trusted context, division of work, human gates, evaluation method, recovery route and a shortlist of candidate use cases.
