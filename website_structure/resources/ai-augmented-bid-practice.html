How to build an AI-augmented bid practice

Build one useful agent, strengthen the foundations and connect the workflow as evidence grows.

Explore the framework ↓
Why now

Start before the transformation feels complete.

AI agents can now hold a goal, retrieve context, use tools and complete bounded steps across a workflow. Orchestration adds the management layer: it breaks a larger outcome into work, routes that work to specialists and brings it back for evaluation and human decision.

You do not need an enterprise platform or a perfect data estate to begin. Start with one useful pilot in a real workflow. Let it leave stronger foundations behind, then connect the next role as the evidence and controls mature.

The central ideaStart with one useful agent. Earn the right to connect the next.
The route

Build in sequence.

Treat every pilot as a controlled learning system, not a technology demonstration.

Start smallChoose one recurring workflow with a meaningful business consequence.
Make it trustworthyPrepare the context, permissions, tests, decision gates and recovery path.
Connect progressivelyAdd specialist roles only when the handover between them creates measurable value.
Six-pillar architecture

Treat it as a managed practice.

The business sets the outcome. Trusted context forms the foundation. Specialist agents and tools do bounded work. Evaluation, human authority and learning keep the whole system dependable.

01

Business outcome

  • Map the current workflow, including waits, handovers and rework.
  • Name the decision, its owner and the acceptance criteria.
  • Set a baseline for quality, cycle time, effort, risk or capacity.

Leader's questionWhat must improve, and what would make us stop?

02

Trusted context

  • Define approved sources and the order of precedence when they conflict.
  • Remove expired evidence and ambiguous ownership.
  • Record source, version and evidence trail with every output.

Leader's questionWhat may the system trust, access and reuse?

03

Agents and tools

  • Give every specialist a clear purpose, inputs, tools, output and limits.
  • Use APIs for structured actions and controlled system access.
  • Prefer a fixed rule where conventional automation is safer and simpler.

Leader's questionWhy an agent, and when must it hand back?

04

Evaluate and refine

  • Test ordinary work, edge cases, conflicting sources and bad inputs.
  • Compare with the current baseline, not a perfect ideal.
  • Separate model error from weak process, missing context and unclear instructions.

Leader's questionHow will we know it is wrong before the work advances?

05

Human decision gates

  • Classify actions by consequence, reversibility and confidence required.
  • Set approval thresholds, decision owners and escalation routes.
  • Provide a clear way to stop, correct and recover the workflow.

Leader's questionWho decides, and what happens when conditions change?

06

Measure and learn

  • Track quality, rework, review burden, cycle time and exceptions.
  • Turn repeated corrections into updates to source, role, test or policy.
  • Choose deliberately: stop, refine, repeat, connect or scale.

Leader's questionHas the workflow earned more scope?

The building analogy

Strong systems need foundations.

Think of the practice as a house. Attractive automation cannot compensate for weak foundations. If sources conflict, access is unreliable or authority is vague, adding more agents usually multiplies the uncertainty.

  1. 01
    The business outcome is the brief. It defines what the system is being built to achieve.
  2. 02
    Trusted context is the foundation. Sources, permissions and integrations give the work somewhere reliable to stand.
  3. 03
    Specialist agents are the trades. Each has a bounded role, tools and a clear handover.
  4. 04
    Evaluation and people provide inspection and sign-off. Measurement becomes the blueprint for the next build.
The first pilot

Choose a Goldilocks use case.

The best first pilot is neither trivial nor transformational. It is repeatable enough to structure, important enough to matter and bounded enough to evaluate. Turn it into one sentence before discussing tools or platforms.

When [named user] needs to [job or decision], the workflow will [bounded agent contribution] using [authorised context], before [named person] decides [human gate].
Recurring

Does this happen often enough to learn from?

Consequential

Would improvement matter to growth, risk or capacity?

Grounded

Are the inputs available, authorised and current?

Evaluable

Can a knowledgeable person judge success?

Controllable

Can it be tested safely before live reliance?

Build progressively

Six controlled moves.

Prove one role, strengthen the foundations and add coordination only when it solves a real constraint.

01 · Choose

One workflow

Name the bottleneck, outcome, owner, baseline and non-negotiable controls.

02 · Prepare

A trusted workspace

Assemble approved sources, permissions, rules, expected outputs and representative cases.

03 · Build

One specialist

Give the agent one bounded role and the minimum tools it needs. Keep the first handover to a person.

04 · Test

Shadow mode

Run beside the existing process. Capture accuracy, completeness, exceptions, review effort and recovery.

05 · Connect

A second role

Add another specialist only when the handover between roles creates measurable value.

06 · Orchestrate

The workflow

Coordinate roles when sequencing, shared state and routing become the constraint.

Apply it to your practice

Map the first credible pilot.

Bring one workflow and the business decision it must improve. We can identify the operating-model gap, the smallest useful agent role and the controls required before the work advances.
