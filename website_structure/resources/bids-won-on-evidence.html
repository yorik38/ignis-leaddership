Bids are now won on evidence, not hours

Why the bid desk, not the pipeline, has become the constraint for UK solar and battery IPPs, and how an agent-supported workflow lets a senior team bid more rounds with the people it has.

Abstract

UK onshore solar and battery developers now win revenue through a chain of competitions, rather than a single auction. That chain is a Contracts for Difference (CfD) round, a route-to-market or corporate PPA, a battery toll or floor, a Capacity Market auction and a Gate 2 grid offer.

Using public results from AR7a, the AR8 notices, the Capacity Market and NESO's connections reform, this paper argues that the binding constraint for a growth-stage IPP is no longer the number of projects. It is the senior time needed to turn one body of project evidence into many consistent, well-priced bids.

An illustrative model shows the senior team getting twice the time on what wins the deal, with fewer hours in total. A worked solar and battery example, also clearly labelled as illustrative, shows what a single pricing or timing decision is worth.

The paper sets out where that time and value are lost, why conventional bid desks break at portfolio scale, and an eight-step workflow. AI agents do the first drafts and cross-checks. Named people own every price, risk and sign-off.

Key findings
1

Price discipline is tightening.

AR7a cleared 4.9 GW of solar across 157 projects at £65.23/MWh, 13% below the administrative strike price. For AR8, government will see anonymised bids and set budgets only after applications are assessed. 13

2

Revenue is assembled from several bids, not one.

CfD, PPA, battery tolls and floors, the Capacity Market and Gate 2 each have their own rules and counterparties. All of them draw on the same project evidence. 12131517

3

The grid queue has become a deadline.

Reform cut a queue of over 700 GW to a 283 GW pipeline. A Gate 2 transmission offer must be signed within 90 days, and some distribution offers within 30. The grid date now drives every other bid. 81120

4

Merchant price signals are weaker than they were.

The T-4 Capacity Market price fell from £60 to £27.10/kW/year. In GB PPAs, buyers' best bids sit £5–10/MWh below sellers' offers, and no transactable price was observed. 1214

5

Twice the time on what wins the deal, and still fewer hours.

In our illustrative model, agents take on coordination, drafting and reconciliation. Senior time on judgement, relationships, strategy and negotiation doubles, while total senior hours per bid fall by about 30%. A 12-project IPP's bid load drops from 121% of a four-person desk's capacity to about 84%.

Key terms
Contracts for Difference (CfD)
UK scheme paying a generator the gap between a fixed strike price and a market reference price. From AR7, contracts for wind and solar run 20 years and are CPI-indexed. 6
Allocation round (AR7, AR7a, AR8)
A competitive CfD auction. Projects bid sealed strike prices by pot, and successful projects receive the clearing price for their pot. 13
Administrative strike price (ASP)
The maximum strike price a technology can receive in a round. For solar it is £75/MWh in 2024 prices for both AR7 and AR8. 3
Gate 2
The stage of GB connections reform at which a project that is ready and strategically aligned receives a firm connection offer with a confirmed date. 8
Tolling agreement
A battery contract in which an offtaker pays a fixed fee for dispatch rights. The owner keeps responsibility for construction and availability. 15
Floor (BESS)
A contract guaranteeing a minimum battery revenue while the owner keeps upside above it. 17
1 · The landscape

What has changed in UK solar and battery bidding since AR7?

Short answer

Three things. CfD rounds now reward tighter pricing: solar cleared 13% below its cap in AR7a, and AR8 adds government sight of bids. Offtake is split across PPAs, tolls, floors and the Capacity Market. And grid reform has turned connection dates into hard deadlines. 138

CfD: more capacity, sharper prices

AR7a, the onshore and solar half of the seventh round, awarded 4,905 MW of solar to 157 projects and 1,306 MW of onshore wind to 28 projects. Every successful solar project received £65.23/MWh in 2024 prices, against an administrative strike price of £75. Onshore wind cleared at £72.24 against £92. 1 Across AR7 as a whole, government reported 14.7 GW from 201 projects. 2

The results table also shows who is winning. We parsed all 157 solar awards. The median project is 29.9 MW, 84 of the 157 projects are 30 MW or smaller, and 24 sit between 49 and 50 MW. 134 are in England. 1 For a growth-stage IPP this is the relevant competition: many mid-sized projects bidding at one clearing price, where a few pounds per MWh separate a contract from a lost year.

AR8 changes the rules again. The Price and Pot Notice puts solar alone in Pot 1 and onshore wind in Pot 2. It keeps the AR7 strike price caps, gives government sight of anonymised sealed bids in Pots 1–4, and states that budgets will be confirmed only after applications have been assessed, with value for money as a key test. 3 The application window ran from 20 July to 7 August 2026, with Pot 1 delivery years from 2028/29 to 2030/31. 4 NESO's Notice of Auction and the Contract Budget Notice are scheduled for 19 November 2026. 5

Offtake: a thinner, more demanding PPA market

Veyt recorded 27 UK PPAs totalling 1.8 GW in 2025, down 0.5 GW year on year. Across Europe, corporate PPA activity fell 19% while route-to-market PPAs tripled to 15 contracts. 13 Pexapark counted 24 GB PPAs (626 MW) announced by October 2025, a 39% fall. It reports sell-side offers for 10-year pay-as-produced solar of £74–84/MWh, while buyers' best bids sit £5–10 lower, so no transactable price was observed. 14 The two databases count differently, which is why their totals differ. Both point the same way.

Batteries: tolls, floors and a cheaper Capacity Market

Battery revenue is being contracted in new ways. Drax signed a 10-year, CPI-indexed toll for Fidra's 250 MW / 500 MWh West Burton project, subject to final investment decision (FID) by Q3 2026, 15 and a 15-year, non-indexed toll for Zenobē's 200 MW / 800 MWh Coalburn site. 16 In both, the owner keeps construction and availability risk. Gresham House signed a revenue floor with Statkraft and a revenue swap with Nephila Climate and Markel, together securing £35m a year across 789 MW. Modo estimates the floor at about £44k/MW/year. 17

The Capacity Market sends a weaker signal than before. The 2029/30 T-4 auction cleared at £27.10/kW/year, down from £60 the previous year. It awarded 40.1 GW of de-rated capacity, of which batteries took 1.2 GW (3.0%). 12

Grid: from queue to deadline

NESO's reform replaced a first-come queue of over 700 GW, about four times projected 2030 need, with a 283 GW pipeline of generation and storage. 8 Of that, 132 GW is aligned with Clean Power 2030 (Phase 1) and a further 151 GW is needed by 2035 (Phase 2). 9 By 10 June 2026, 713 of the 1,223 projects in the pre-2030 pipeline (58%) had received offers, totalling 37 GW. 10 NESO gives 90 days to sign a Gate 2 transmission offer, 11 while National Grid Electricity Distribution gives 30 days for its Gate 2 variation offers. 20 Phase 2 transmission offers run from early September 2026 to mid-January 2027, 18 and Ofgem has set 31 January 2027 as the deadline for issuing them. 19 Clean Power 2030 still needs solar to roughly triple and batteries to grow about fivefold. 7 The volume is there; access to it is now rationed by readiness and by dates.

2 · Analysis

Where are bid time and value lost?

Short answer

Mostly before and between documents, not in them. Time goes on late bid or no-bid calls, evidence rebuilt for each bid, pricing models that drift from the latest inputs, inconsistencies between submissions, and lessons that are never written down. Each of these costs margin as well as hours.

There is no public dataset on bid hours in UK renewables, so this section is analysis rather than measurement. It draws on how the competitions above are structured and on Ignis practitioner experience. We see five recurring loss points. Each one is a place where senior time goes into production rather than judgement, or where an error reaches the counterparty.

1

Late bid or no-bid call

Teams start drafting before the price floor, grid date and partner position are agreed. Weeks are spent on bids that should have been declined.

CostWasted bid cost, and senior focus taken off the bids that matter
2

Evidence rebuilt each time

Land, planning, grid, yield and cost evidence is gathered again for each CfD, PPA or toll pack.

CostHours lost, and stale inputs in front of buyers and lenders
3

Pricing drift

The bid model, board paper and counterparty term sheet use different capex or yield assumptions.

CostBids priced below viability, or a contract won at a loss
4

Inconsistent submissions

Delivery years, capacity and grid dates differ across CfD, PPA and Gate 2 documents.

CostClarification questions, credibility loss and delivery-year risk
5

Lessons not captured

Debriefs and clearing data are not fed back into the next bid.

CostThe same mistakes repeated across rounds

The pattern is consistent. The scarce people, usually the CEO, the commercial director and one or two senior managers, spend most of their bid time on chasing inputs, drafting and reconciling documents. Judgement, buyer and partner relationships, strategy and negotiation are the work that wins the deal, and they get whatever time is left over. 3 · Analysis

Why do conventional bid desks break at portfolio scale?

Short answer

Because the number of bid events grows with projects multiplied by revenue streams, while the number of senior people stays flat. A 12-project IPP can face dozens of CfD, PPA, toll, Capacity Market and Gate 2 events a year, many in the same months. The desk runs out of senior hours before it runs out of projects.

A single-asset developer can run one bid at a time. A fund-backed IPP with 5 to 20 projects cannot. Each project may need a CfD application, a route-to-market or corporate PPA, a battery toll or floor, Capacity Market prequalification and a Gate 2 acceptance, and these deadlines overlap. In 2026 alone, the AR8 window, the T-4 results, a published battery-toll FID deadline and the Gate 2 offer wave all fell within a few months of each other (Figure 8).

Three things break first. Key people: one commercial director holds the price logic for every bid. Consistency: the same project is described differently to DESNZ, a PPA buyer, a toll counterparty and NESO. Learning: when the desk is over capacity, the debrief is the first thing to go. Hiring helps only slowly. A senior bid manager who knows CfD rules, PPA terms and lender requirements takes months to find and longer to bring up to speed.

4 · The workflow

How does an agentic bid workflow work?

Short answer

It runs every bid through the same eight steps on one shared body of evidence. AI agents read the rules, assemble evidence, produce first drafts and cross-check numbers. A named person makes each decision and signs it off at a gate. The output is a bid, plus a record that the next bid can reuse.

The design principle is borrowed from good investment committees: separate the preparation from the decision, and keep both on the record. Agents are good at reading long rule books, comparing documents and drafting to a template. People are needed for price, risk appetite, partner terms and anything that commits the company. The eight steps in Figure 9 apply whether the bid is a CfD application, a PPA tender, a toll, a Capacity Market entry or a Gate 2 acceptance.

Under the workflow sits a deliberately boring stack: one evidence library per project, one pricing model per project linked to that library, a template set for each bid type, and an access and audit log. Ignis agents provide the agent layer for this work. The Ignis system is in pilot, and we make no performance claims for it here. The design is meant to be useful on any toolset.

What stays human

Price, walk-away, partner terms, risk allocation, anything that commits capital, and the final sign-off. If an agent output would change any of these, it goes to a named person first.

5 · Risk and value

How should an IPP value a bid before writing it?

Short answer

Treat each bid as an option. Its value is the chance of winning multiplied by the margin if you win, less the bid cost and the expected cost of errors. In a pay-as-clear CfD round, bidding lower changes your chance of winning, not the price you receive, so the floor price matters more than the bid price.

Each term has an owner. P(award) depends on how competitive the price is and how strong the evidence is. Contract margin depends on the strike or contract price, yield, capex and tenor. Bid cost is mostly senior time. P(error) × cost of error is the term most teams leave out. It covers a delivery year the grid date cannot meet, a capex input that is six months out of date, or a PPA offer that contradicts the CfD bid.

Two consequences follow. First, in a CfD round all successful projects in a pot receive the clearing price, 1 so the highest-value pricing work is setting an honest floor from current capex, yield and grid evidence, not shaving the bid. Second, the cheapest way to raise expected value is usually to cut the error term with cross-checks, rather than to add more drafting hours.

6 · Worked example

What is a solar and battery bid worth getting right?

Short answer

In our illustrative 60 MW solar case, £1/MWh on the strike price is worth about £58,000 a year, or about £0.6m in present value over a 20-year CfD. A one-year slip in the grid date defers about £3.8m of revenue. The pricing floor and the grid date matter far more than drafting polish.

The example is a hypothetical co-located project: 60 MW of solar and a 30 MW / 60 MWh battery. The team is considering an AR8 CfD for the solar plus a route-to-market PPA, a toll or floor for the battery, and a Capacity Market entry. Public numbers anchor the case. 7 · Implications

What should IPP CEOs do now?

Short answer

Decide which competitions you will enter for each project, and set the floor price before any drafting starts. Put each project's evidence in one place. Make cross-checks a step that bids cannot skip. Hand drafting and checking to agents under named owners, and measure senior hours on judgement rather than on production.

  1. Set a portfolio bid calendar. List every CfD, PPA, toll, Capacity Market and Gate 2 event for the next 12 months against the people available. Decline early where load exceeds capacity.
  2. Fix the floor before the bid. Agree each project's walk-away on current capex, yield and grid evidence at the bid or no-bid gate, not in the final week.
  3. One project, one evidence library. Every bid draws on the same approved facts. Stale items are flagged automatically.
  4. Make cross-checks mandatory. No submission leaves until dates, capacity and prices match the model and every other live submission.
  5. Measure the right hours. Track senior time on judgement, relationships and negotiation against time on coordination and production. Aim to double the first while cutting the total. Move production work to agents with named reviewers.

For investors, the outcome is a bid record they can audit: why each bid was entered, at what floor, on which evidence and with whose sign-off. That record supports the board pack and the lender pack at financial close.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does AI write the bid?

Agents write first drafts and check them against the evidence and the model. People write the story, set the price and sign off. Nothing is submitted without a named owner's approval.

Does this apply to PPA, battery and grid bids, or only CfD?

All of them. The same eight steps apply to CfD applications, PPA tenders, battery tolls and floors, Capacity Market entries and Gate 2 acceptances, because they all draw on the same project evidence.

How quickly can a team start?

In about 30 days. Review a recent bid, map the next 12 months of events, design the library and gates, then run one live bid or no-bid decision through the workflow.

Appendix A

Is your bid desk ready for portfolio scale?

Short answer

Score eight statements. Seven or more means the desk can take on more rounds. Three or fewer means the bid or no-bid gate is the first fix.

0/8 Tick what is true today
7–8 Ready to scale: bid more rounds
4–6 Fix the gaps before AR8 and the next Gate 2 wave
0–3 Bid fewer, better: start with the bid or no-bid gate
Appendix B

Questions to ask your team on Monday

  1. Which bid events fall in the next 90 days, and who owns each one?
  2. What is our floor price for each, and when was it last tested?
  3. Where does the same project appear with different dates or numbers?
  4. How many senior hours went on production rather than judgement, relationships and negotiation last month?
  5. What did we learn from the last round, and where is it written down?
