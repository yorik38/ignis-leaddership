Global energy markets.

How global gas and LNG trade, electricity prices and long-term contracts affect clean power in Britain and Europe.

All resources

Gas and LNG link regions

Natural gas moves between countries by pipeline or as liquefied natural gas (LNG) by ship. When demand rises in one region or supply falls, buyers can compete for the same flexible cargoes. That is one way a disruption far from Europe can feed through to European gas prices. Storage levels, weather and import capacity affect how exposed each market is.

When gas sets the power price

In a day-ahead electricity auction, buyers and sellers make offers for power to be delivered the next day. In a pay-as-clear market, the cost of serving the last increment of demand helps determine the clearing price for each period and bidding zone. When a gas plant is needed on that margin, its fuel and carbon costs can move the power price. Strong wind or solar output can change which plants are needed.

Interconnectors share power across borders

Cross-border cables let systems trade electricity. An interconnected market can use surplus wind, solar or hydro from one area to meet demand in another. If the cable reaches its available capacity, prices can diverge across the two areas. That is why a European average can hide what is happening in an individual market.

Long-term contracts shape investment

A power purchase agreement sets terms for a buyer to purchase electricity from a generator over time. Britain’s Contracts for Difference scheme uses a strike price and a market reference price. The generator can receive a top-up when the reference price is lower and pay back the difference when it is higher. Both approaches can give a project more predictable revenue.
